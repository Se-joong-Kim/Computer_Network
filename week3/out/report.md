## 1. The rule I used for "third party"

**Definition.** A site is *third-party delivered* when its content comes off infrastructure that a different organisation operates. This is deliberately not "somebody else hosts the DNS": Netflix's DNS is served by AWS Route 53 while its video comes off Netflix's own Open Connect appliances, and calling that third-party delivery would be wrong.

**"Same domain" means the registrable domain (eTLD+1) under the Public Suffix List, not the last two labels.** For `www.bbc.co.uk` the last two labels are `co.uk`, which is a public suffix nobody owns.

The rules, applied in order:

| # | Signal | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Chain's final registrable domain is in the curated CDN-operator list | third party, operator named |
| R2 | Chain left the origin domain, **but** the two zones publish the identical NS RRset, or the address PTRs back into the origin, or the pair is in the same-organisation map | not third party (own infrastructure under a second brand domain) |
| R3 | Chain left the origin domain and R1/R2 both missed | third party, **operator unclassified** — flagged as low confidence rather than guessed |
| R0/R4 | **No CNAME leaves the origin.** Do not stop here — look at the addresses: do the resolvers disagree? does the PTR land in a CDN or cloud domain? | different address sets or a cloud PTR ⇒ third-party edge reached without a CNAME; one identical set with an own-domain PTR ⇒ not a CDN |

**A weakness I want to declare.** The NS signal in R2 compares the *exact* NS RRset, not the nameserver provider. `github.com` uses `dns*.p08.nsone.net` and `netlifyglobalcdn.com` uses `dns*.p02.nsone.net` — both are NS1 customers. A rule that read "same DNS provider ⇒ same organisation" would fuse GitHub and Netlify into one company. Comparing the exact RRset separates them (`p08` ≠ `p02`), but the near-miss is real and worth naming.

### `www.wikipedia.org` — naive false positive

Naive says **third party**; my rule says **not third party (own infrastructure)**.

Evidence: wikipedia.org and wikimedia.org publish the identical NS RRset (ns0/ns1/ns2.wikimedia.org), and the address PTRs to text-lb.*.wikimedia.org


### Right answer, wrong reason — the public-suffix bug

- `www.korea.ac.kr`: the naive rule's "domain" is `ac.kr`, which is a **public suffix**, not a registrable domain. It got the verdict right by luck.
- `www.bbc.co.uk`: the naive rule's "domain" is `co.uk`, which is a **public suffix**, not a registrable domain. It got the verdict right by luck.

  Counterfactual: had `www.korea.ac.kr` been CNAMEd to a third-party edge inside the same public suffix — say `edge.somecdn.ac.kr` — the naive rule would compare `ac.kr` with `ac.kr`, call them equal, and report "not third party". Using the Public Suffix List makes my rule immune to this.


### Right answer, but only because the chain was followed to the end

- `www.apple.com`: the first CNAME hop is `www-apple-com.v.aaplimg.com`, and `aaplimg.com` is owned by the same organisation. A rule that judged on the *first* hop would have called this third-party delivery on the strength of a domain the site owns. The genuine third party only appears at `akamaiedge.net`, further down the chain.

## 5. Does this support claim (b)?

**Partly — and the honest answer is more interesting than a yes.**

Where DNS clearly does steer: the Akamai-fronted names return a different address to almost every resolver, and my own Task 1 walk (which asks the authoritative servers directly, from Seoul) lands on a Korean Akamai POP. That is DNS-based replica selection working exactly as the lecture describes.

Where it does not, and why the number above overstates the case:

1. **The public resolvers are all anycast.** Queried from Seoul, `8.8.8.8`, `9.9.9.9` and `1.1.1.1` all land on nearby Asian POPs. They are not three vantage points; they are roughly one. You cannot manufacture geographic distance by picking a US-branded IP address.

2. **Much of the variance is ECS policy, not geography.** Google sends EDNS Client Subnet; Quad9 and Cloudflare deliberately do not. So a CDN that steers on ECS will answer Google differently from Quad9 for reasons that have nothing to do with where the *client* is.

3. **Some sites are steered by BGP, not DNS.** Any site whose address is identical from every resolver but whose PTR lands on an anycast platform is having its replica chosen in the routing layer. Counting only "different address ⇒ DNS steering works" would silently credit DNS for BGP's work, and counting "same address ⇒ steering failed" would be just as wrong.

   - `www.stanford.edu`: one address set from every resolver; PTR `afa7f374f51cc8991.awsglobalaccelerator.com` — replica selection is happening below DNS.

`task2.md` says it is allowed not to support claim (b). The measurement supports the *mechanism* and does not, from a single network, establish the *proximity* half of it.


## 6. Limitations

- **One network only.** This changes the question from "does DNS steer by *where I am*" to "does DNS steer by *which resolver I ask*". Those are different claims and only the second is tested here. The steering number is therefore a **lower bound**.
- Anycast-fronted sites are invisible to this method entirely — their replica choice never appears in a DNS answer.
- `dig` is not installed on this machine, so every lookup here runs on the DNS wire implementation written for Task 1 (`task1_resolve.DNSClient`), over UDP/53 with a TCP fallback. Task 1's `dig_answer()` similarly falls back to `socket.getaddrinfo`, which asks the same recursive resolver `dig +short` would.
- CDN mappings rotate. Every table above is generated from `out/chains.json` at report time rather than typed by hand, so re-running `--collect` and `--report` regenerates the whole document consistently.
- `ttl_min` is recorded per resolver in `chains.json` but not analysed here; a short TTL is itself evidence of active steering.

## 7. Part A — the capture

`out/dns.pcapng` — 38 packets (19 queries, 19 responses), captured while resolving `www.adobe.com`, `www.stanford.edu`.

**A2 — a query and its response, matched by transaction ID**

Frame **1** is a query for `www.adobe.com` sent to `198.41.0.4` with transaction ID **0x7453**; frame **2** is the response from `198.41.0.4` carrying the same ID **0x7453**. The ID is the only thing tying them together — DNS over UDP has no connection state.

**A3 — a delegation and an answer, the same packet format**

Frame **2** (838 bytes, from `198.41.0.4`) is a **delegation**: `ANCOUNT=0`, and the authority section holds 13 `NS` records for `com` with 27 additional records as glue. Nothing in the answer section at all.

Frame **10** (60 bytes, from `193.108.91.67`) is an **answer**: `ANCOUNT=1`, with the `A` record in the answer section and `AA=1`.

They are the *same message format*. A delegation is not a different kind of packet — it is the identical header and section layout with the answer section left empty and the authority section filled in. That is the whole point of A3, and it is much more obvious on the wire than in parsed text.

**A4 — the largest response**

**854 bytes**, frame 34, from `198.41.0.4` — the `com` delegation from a root server. It is large because it carries 13 `NS` records *and* 27 additional records: an `A` and an `AAAA` glue record for almost every one of those nameservers. A delegation has to be self-contained, otherwise the resolver could not reach the servers it just named.

  Measured, by asking `198.41.0.4` for `www.adobe.com` twice: **without EDNS0 the same response is 491 bytes with `TC=1`** and only 11 additional records — the server had to drop most of the glue and tell us to come back over TCP. Advertising a 1232-byte EDNS0 buffer gets 838 bytes, `TC=0`, and all 27 additional records in one UDP datagram. The 512-byte limit in RFC 1035 is why EDNS0 exists.
